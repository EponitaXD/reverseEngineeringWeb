What is Reverse Engineering?

Reverse engineering is the systematic process of analyzing an existing product, system, or piece of software to understand how it works, how it was designed, and how its components interact. Rather than starting from requirements and building forward, reverse engineering works backward from the finished artifact to uncover its structure, functionality, and underlying principles. This process is commonly used in engineering, computer science, and cybersecurity to study legacy systems, improve or redesign products, ensure interoperability, or identify vulnerabilities. By deconstructing a system into its individual parts—whether hardware components, software modules, or data flows—engineers can gain insight into design decisions that may not be documented or easily observable.

The reverse engineering process typically involves several stages: observation, decomposition, analysis, and reconstruction. First, the system is observed in operation to understand its behavior and outputs. Next, it is broken down into smaller components that can be individually examined. During analysis, engineers determine how each component functions and how they communicate with one another, often creating diagrams, models, or specifications to capture this understanding. Finally, the knowledge gained is used to reconstruct the system conceptually or to develop an improved or alternative design. Overall, reverse engineering is a powerful tool for learning from existing solutions, maintaining and upgrading complex systems, and fostering innovation by building upon what already exists.

Stages of Reverse Engineering:

1. Reference Material / Prior Work

Why start from scratch when you can utilize what is known?

  • Search online for information about the device you want to reverse engineer.
  • Compile useful resources for future reference.
  • Store links and documentation in a dedicated reference section.

2. Preparation & Imaging

Important: Document how the device fits together so it can be rebuilt.

  • High-Resolution Photos: Capture the exterior, ports, and internal PCB.
  • Inventory: List all visible chips and part numbers.
  • Safety Check: Identify the power source and ensure the device is powered off before working.

3. Hardware Exploration (Teardown)

  • Identify Components: Look up datasheets for each chip to understand its function.
  • Find Test Points: Locate pads labeled GND, VCC, TX, or RX for debugging access.

4. Data Extraction (Digital Forensics Lite)

  • Port Scanning: Connect via USB or Ethernet to see how the device is recognized.
  • UART / Serial Access: Use a USB-to-TTL adapter to monitor communication through TX/RX pins.

5. Analysis & Software Reverse Engineering

Ghidra is a free, open-source software reverse engineering suite used for binary analysis and decompilation.

  • Code Review: Analyze firmware by loading it into Ghidra.
  • Visual Mapping: Create block diagrams showing component connections.

6. Testing & Feedback Loop

  • Observation: Modify connections or settings and observe behavior changes.
  • Verification: Test hypotheses (e.g., power a component to confirm its function).

7. Personal Documentation

  • Technical Notes: Record successes and failures.
  • Final Report: Summarize findings and share with others if desired.
